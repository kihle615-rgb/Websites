About us

Fragrance is the shortest road back to a memory.

In the Middle East a fragrance is not an afterthought on the way out the door. It is offered to guests, layered for an occasion, and worn close enough that people remember you by it years later.

Son of the Middle East Fragrances brings that tradition to Gqeberha. We are a fragrance and aroma supplier in the Dolphins Leap Centre on Humewood Road, stocking oud, amber, rose and musk built in the concentrations they are meant to be worn at. Nothing here is designed to disappear by lunchtime.

We are open nine in the morning until nine at night, every day of the week — so there is no rush. Come in after work, take your time, and smell as many as you like.

Smelling it properly is the only real way to choose a fragrance. That is why we would rather you visited than guessed from a screen.

    The history of perfume

    Four thousand years of trying to bottle it.

    Almost everything modern perfumery does was worked out in the Middle East first — the still, the extraction of rose, the trade routes that made resin worth more than gold. Here is the short version.

    1. c. 1200 BCE

      Mesopotamia

      A cuneiform tablet records Tapputi-Belatekallim, a palace perfumer who distilled flowers, oil and calamus, filtered the result and returned it to the still. She is the first chemist anywhere named in the historical record — and she was making perfume.

    2. c. 1500 BCE

      Ancient Egypt

      Temples burn kyphi, a compound incense of resin, honey and wine. Scent is a religious instrument and a burial rite, and Queen Hatshepsut sends an expedition to Punt to bring back living myrrh trees.

    3. Antiquity onward

      Southern Arabia

      The incense route runs frankincense and myrrh north out of what is now Yemen and Oman. The resin is so valuable it is traded against gold, and whole cities are built on the strength of it.

    4. 9th century

      Baghdad

      Al-Kindi writes the Book of the Chemistry of Perfume and Distillations — more than a hundred recipes for scented oils, salves and aromatic waters, along with the equipment to make them. It is the first perfumery manual worth the name.

    5. 11th century

      Persia

      Ibn Sina, known in Europe as Avicenna, refines steam distillation to draw the oil out of rose petals without scorching them. That technique is still how attar is made a thousand years later.

    6. Then as now

      South & South-East Asia

      Oud is the exception to everything. Aquilaria trees only produce the dark, resinous heartwood when a mould infects them — most trees never do. That accident of biology is why oud remains one of the most expensive raw materials on earth, and why it sits at the base of so much Middle Eastern perfumery.

    7. 14th century

      Europe

      Alcohol-based perfume arrives, remembered as Hungary Water. It is lighter, it carries further, and it fades faster — the split between European eau and Eastern attar starts here and has never quite closed.

    8. Today

      Summerstrand, Gqeberha

      The same materials, on Humewood Road. Come and smell what four thousand years of practice produced.

    How a scent unfolds

    Top, heart, base.

    A fragrance is not one smell — it is three, arriving in order. What you smell on the card in the first ten seconds is the part that leaves first. This is why we ask you to wear it a while before deciding.

    1. 01

      Top

      First 15 minutes

      The opening. Citrus, pepper, fruit — bright, sharp, and gone quickly. It is the introduction, not the fragrance.

    2. 02

      Heart

      20 minutes to 2 hours

      The body of it. Rose, jasmine, spice. This is the character you will actually wear for most of the day.

    3. 03

      Base

      2 to 8 hours and beyond

      What stays. Oud, amber, musk, sandalwood — the part that settles into fabric and skin, and the part people remember.
